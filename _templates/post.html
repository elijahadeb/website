gate −1 — three dot products and a number

08.28.26 · pre-ignition · 6 min · session —

before day one is allowed to exist, the plan demands one artifact: a four-hour, by-hand c++ test. mmap two 100m-float files, take their dot product three ways — scalar, avx2, threaded — and print a bandwidth figure in gb/s. not a program. a number.

the point of the exercise is the shape of the curve, not the code. scalar shows the floor. vectorizing buys a multiple. threads buy the rest — and then the line goes flat, because on this machine inference is a negotiation with dram, not with the alu.

why this one comes before day one

everything in year one is priced against how fast i can write correct low-level c++. if that assumption is wrong, every bar downstream is wrong with it, and i'd rather find out in an afternoon than in november. so this gate isn't really about dot products. it's a calibration test with a consequence attached, and the consequence is written down before the run:

result                        consequence
correct, under 4 hours        every q1 bar stands as written
correct, but over 4 hours     q1 speed bar drops from 1/3 to 1/4 of llama.cpp
could not finish              three-week c++ ramp goes in front of q1

rules: one sitting, four-hour timebox, docs allowed, no ai-generated code. that last one matters more than the timebox. an llm writes a correct avx2 kernel in ninety seconds, which tells me nothing about whether i can debug one at 2am in january.

what this gate is not measuring

two 100m-float32 arrays is 800 mb. this laptop has 16gb of ram. after the first pass both files sit in page cache and never touch the ssd again. that's deliberate — this measures dram, not storage. the disk ceiling is a different number with a different harness, and it's roughly an order of magnitude lower.

i'm saying it out loud because mmap in the description makes this look like a storage test, and it isn't. the two ceilings are far enough apart that confusing them is the fastest way to publish something wrong. what i will do is time the first pass separately — that one does touch the ssd, and it's a free preview of the number that matters more.

what i expect, written before i run it

if i only report results, nobody can tell whether i understood the machine or fit a story to it afterward.

scalar — one multiply-add per pair, no vectorization. the floor. cpu-bound, nowhere near the memory ceiling.

avx2 — 8 floats per instruction with fma. the big jump lands here. not 8×, because past some point the arithmetic stops being the problem and the loads become the problem.

threaded — this is the one i expect to disappoint, and i want that on record. a dot product reads 8 bytes and does one fma. there is almost no arithmetic per byte moved. so threads should saturate dram at two or three cores and then flatten. if threaded is dramatically faster than avx2, either my avx2 was bad or i'm measuring cache. that flattening is the point of the whole gate — it's the first time i see the wall i'm going to spend a year arguing with.

the trap, which is not performance

i went in thinking the hard part was speed. it isn't. it's the sum.

100m products of u(0,1) values average about 0.25 each, so the true dot product is around 2.5e7. a single float32 accumulator holds about 7 decimal digits. once that accumulator passes roughly 4.2e6, the gap between representable neighbours exceeds 0.5 — and adding 0.25 to it rounds to nothing at all. the sum stops growing. not drifts. stops.

true dot product (float64 ref)        ≈ 2.5e7
single float32 accumulator saturates  ≈ 4.2e6   →  ~17% of the true value

that isn't a rounding wobble, it's an 83% error, and it fails the 1e-3 bar by four orders of magnitude. the consequence is the interesting part: more parallelism makes it more accurate. eight avx2 lanes each carry an eighth of the sum, so each one peaks near 3.1e6 — still not safe. unroll four deep for 32 accumulators and each holds about 7.8e5, comfortably clear. the vectorized version isn't just faster than naive scalar, it's more correct, and the scalar baseline needs a float64 accumulator to be a fair reference at all.

the whole effect depends on the data. with u(-1,1) the sum is a random walk landing near 3,300 and none of this happens. so the generator distribution is part of the specification, not an implementation detail. mine is u(0,1), seeded, and the generator ships in the repo. disclosure: this trap was pointed out to me before the run rather than discovered during it. the timebox still tests intrinsics, threading, mmap and debugging under pressure — but i didn't find this one myself.

three gaps, three lessons. scalar → avx2 is what vectorizing is worth. threaded → dram measured is how much of the ceiling i actually reached. dram measured → theoretical is the difference between what the spec sheet promises and what the silicon delivers. and the cold row, sitting far below all of them, is the year.

reproduce this

one clone, one make, one run. thirty minutes on any x86-64 linux box with avx2. if your numbers disagree with mine, email me — law 1 says mine don't exist yet.

git clone https://github.com/elijahadeb/dead-silicon-bench
cd dead-silicon-bench && make gate-minus-1
./gate-minus-1 --generate --seed 0
./gate-minus-1 a.f32 b.f32

your bandwidth will differ from mine. the shape should not: scalar low, avx2 high, threaded flat. if your threaded row keeps climbing past three cores, tell me — that means i'm wrong about where the wall is, and i'd like to know.
